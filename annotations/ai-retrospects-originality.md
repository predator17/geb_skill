# Unit: Chapter XVIII — Artificial Intelligence: Retrospects (Originality and Problem Reduction)
**PDF pages**: 649–654
**Printed folios**: ~606–611

## Key terms
- **author vs. meta-author**: The program that produces a result versus the human who wrote the program. Credit for originality is split according to how deeply the discovery is buried in the program’s mechanisms.
- **problem reduction**: The recursive technique of breaking a global goal into subgoals, sub-subgoals, and so on, until the remaining tasks are elementary. Can produce infinite goal stacks (Zeno) or succeed when the problem space is represented well.
- **problem space**: The way a problem is internally represented; different representations turn the same physical situation into reducing steps or magnifying steps (the dog-and-bone example).

## Concept unpacking
When a program produces a result its programmer never explicitly considered (Gelernter’s geometry program rediscovering the pons asinorum proof; algorithmic music), the question arises whether the program or the programmer deserves credit. If the generating mechanisms are transparent and close to the surface, the programmer remains the real author; if the interaction is complex and opaque, the program can be treated as an author in its own right and the human as meta-author. The same chapter introduces problem reduction as a powerful but double-edged method: it converts global goals into local strategies, yet can generate infinite regresses unless the problem space is represented so that subgoals genuinely advance the original goal.

## Author-stated intentions
To examine the conditions under which a program can be said to exhibit originality, and to show how problem reduction both enables and can derail intelligent search.
